USDA Breach: Hackers Reach the System That Pays Farmers, and Agency Systems Go Offline

Database passwords reset, apps offline and no answers on who did it. The USDA breach as reported by NBC News and Nextgov, with steps for farmers.

Rapid+ Team · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 24 November 2026. This is a developing story based on NBC News (20 November) and Nextgov (19 November). Both rely on unnamed sources, and neither names a group behind the breach. A CNN report on a breach of an FBI database could not be read, so we do not cover it. Details may change as the USDA says more.

The US Department of Agriculture has taken many of its computer systems offline after hackers got into the system that pays farmers. Staff were told to reset every database password, and for days many of the financial tools farmers rely on stopped working. No one has said who did it or whether data was taken. If you farm in the US, or work with the programme, this is what is known so far.

The short version

  • Hackers accessed the USDA system that facilitates payments to farmers, according to NBC News, citing sources. The agency told employees on Thursday, 19 November.
  • Nextgov reports that numerous USDA applications were taken offline over several days, and that outages hit many of the financial tools used for payments between the agency and farmers.
  • An internal email on Monday, 16 November, said the IT team had to "reset all database passwords in all environments", according to Nextgov.
  • It is not clear who carried out the intrusion or whether any data was taken.
  • A bulletin dated 19 November warned that applications might have intermittent outages through the week.

What happened

According to Nextgov, the USDA's Cybersecurity and Privacy Operations Center found a security incident. A message on Monday from an acting assistant CIO at the Farm Production and Conservation Business Center told staff that the Information Services Division had to reset all database passwords across all environments, which in turn forced a reset of every application, causing outages. By Thursday some staff also had trouble with the department's VPN.

NBC News adds that the attack came this week, according to two former USDA officials, and that the department told staff on Thursday that hackers had accessed the system that handles payments to farmers. NBC's reporting is for subscribers and we read only part of it.

Who is affected

Neither article gives a number of farmers. Nextgov says the payments cover things such as crop insurance and losses from bad weather. Staff reported that they could not reach client-facing apps that farmers use to check notifications and the status of loans and other aid. The department's public statements were not available in the pages we read, and Nextgov says that USDA and CISA were asked for comment without a reported reply.

Staff were left guessing

Nextgov's three sources say department leaders have not kept staff fully informed, and it is unclear whether every employee received the notice. That matters, because staff who do not know what happened may reuse old passwords or fall for messages pretending to be IT. In a breach, clear communication is part of the defence.

Why resetting every password is a big step

A reset of all database passwords in all environments is a heavy response. It suggests the team feared that an attacker held credentials that could still be used, and wanted to cut off access everywhere at once. The price is that everything that depends on those passwords breaks until it is reconfigured. That is why so many applications went down. It is also why a reset is the kind of step you only take when you think access may be widespread. The reporting does not confirm how far the intruder reached.

What farmers and agricultural businesses can do

  1. Use official channels. Check the USDA's own website and your local Farm Service Agency office for status. Do not trust messages that arrive by text or email.
  2. Expect phishing. A real outage is a perfect cover for fake messages about "payment problems" or "update your bank details". Hang up on calls that ask for banking information.
  3. Watch your bank account. If you receive USDA payments, check deposits and look for changes to your payment details.
  4. Keep records. Save copies of applications, payment notices and loan paperwork, in case a payment is delayed.
  5. Change passwords for any USDA online account, and on any other site where you used the same one. Turn on two-step verification where it is offered.

The bigger picture

This is the latest in a run of government and infrastructure incidents. A week earlier, a string of big outages had officials assuring the public that they were not cyberattacks. This one is different, because the USDA's own staff and sources describe an outside intrusion. Our report on supplier breaches shows how attackers often reach large organisations through weak links, though nothing in the USDA reporting says how this one happened.

Frequently asked questions

What did hackers access at the USDA?

NBC News reports they accessed the system that facilitates payments to farmers. Nextgov says many financial tools used for those payments were taken offline.

Was farmers' personal data stolen?

Nextgov says it is not clear whether any data was taken. NBC reports concern that hackers could access sensitive information. No confirmation has been given.

Who is behind the attack?

Unknown. Neither article names a group, a country or a person.

Will farmers still get paid?

The articles do not say payments stopped, but they describe outages in the tools used to process and track them. Check with your local USDA service office for your own case.

How should I protect myself?

Use official USDA channels, ignore unexpected messages about payments, watch your bank account, and change passwords on any USDA account.
