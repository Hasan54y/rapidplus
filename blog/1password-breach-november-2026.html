Names, emails and account details for some users were taken. Your passwords were not, the company says. What happened and how to spot the phishing that follows.

Rapid+ Team · 28 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 28 November 2026. This is based on reporting by Help Net Security, MacRumors and The Register, which quote 1Password's own incident update of 25 November. 1Password has not said how many customers were affected or how the employee's GitHub account was taken over, so we do not guess.

1Password, one of the most popular password managers, says an attacker got into an employee's GitHub account, copied private source code and pulled customer account records from its systems. The company insists that the passwords people keep in their vaults were never reached. If you use 1Password, the important part is what was taken, what was not, and why the next few weeks of emails deserve extra suspicion.

The short version

  • On 19 November 2026, 1Password began investigating signs that an attacker had access to an employee's corporate GitHub account. It disclosed the incident on 25 November.
  • The attacker copied source code from private repositories, reached the build system (CI/CD) and pushed malicious code to backend services, which then pulled customer account data.
  • Taken for some users: names, email addresses, account and user identifiers, account creation dates and last sign-in times, plus public keys and other non-secret data.
  • Not taken, according to 1Password: vault contents, the keys that protect vaults and account recovery keys.
  • 1Password says no action is needed on your account. The real risk is phishing that uses the leaked names and emails.

What happened

According to 1Password's update, quoted by Help Net Security, the company "began investigating indications that an attacker gained access to an employee's corporate GitHub account" on 19 November. Using that access, the attacker copied source code from private repositories and got into the continuous integration and delivery system, the machinery that builds and ships the service. The Register reports that the intruder planted malicious code in backend services, which then downloaded customer records.

1Password has not explained how the GitHub account was compromised, or how access to it allowed code to reach backend services. The Register says the company did not answer its questions on those points.

What was taken, and what was not

Taken (for a subset of users)Not taken, says 1Password
Name and email address used with 1PasswordVault contents: your passwords, notes and other items
Account and user identifiersThe keys that protect vaults
Account creation date and last sign-in timeAccount recovery keys
Public keys, which are public by designYour account password and Secret Key, which 1Password does not store
SRP sign-in parameters, which 1Password says cannot be used to access an accountThe apps and browser extensions themselves
Tokens from some invitation, provisioning and recovery emails
Private source code and access to the build system

The company says the stolen tokens are not enough to open an account on their own, because they only work together with a single sign-on login or a code emailed to the user. It contacted the small number of people with invitations or recoveries in progress, and says it found no evidence of account takeovers using those tokens.

How 1Password responded

  • Disabled the compromised GitHub account and revoked the attacker's token.
  • Rolled affected backend services back to clean versions of the code, according to The Register.
  • Rotated or revoked internal credentials that may have been exposed, including secrets kept in the build system.
  • Tightened controls on GitHub, the build system and employee devices, and added new detections.
  • Changed email verification codes from six digits to ten letters and numbers, which are much harder to guess.
  • Brought in outside security experts to check the clean-up.

MacRumors notes that the disclosure came about six days after the investigation began. 1Password has not said how many customers were affected, or whether personal, family or business accounts were involved.

Why your passwords should still be safe

1Password, like most modern password managers, encrypts your vault on your own device with keys derived from your account password and a Secret Key that the company never holds. That design means a stolen copy of server data should still be unreadable. "1Password does not have access to your password or secret keys," the company said, as quoted by Help Net Security. This is also why the leaked SRP parameters matter less than they sound: they are part of how you prove your password without sending it.

The caution is history. The Register points out that LastPass's 2022 breach also began with stolen source code from a development environment, and information from that first intrusion helped a second attack that took encrypted vault backups. 1Password says nothing of the kind happened here, but it is a reason to watch for further updates.

What to do if you use 1Password

  1. Expect phishing. Attackers now know some users' names, emails and when they signed up. Be suspicious of any email, text or call about 1Password, invitations or account recovery.
  2. Never type your account password or Secret Key into a link. 1Password says it will never ask for them. Open the app or type 1password.com yourself.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication for your 1Password account if you have not already, ideally with an authenticator app or security key.
  4. Ignore urgent "account locked" messages. Urgency is the oldest phishing trick, as our guide to spotting fake pages explains.
  5. Consider passkeys for your most important accounts. A passkey will not work on a fake site, which makes phishing far less useful. See our passkey guide.
  6. Stay informed. Read 1Password's own incident page for updates rather than relying on emails that claim to be from the company.

You do not need to change your 1Password account password because of this incident, according to the company. Changing it anyway does no harm if it makes you feel safer.

Frequently asked questions

Were my 1Password passwords stolen?

1Password says no. Vault contents, the keys that protect them and account recovery keys were not accessed.

What customer data was taken?

For a subset of users: names, email addresses, account and user identifiers, account creation and last sign-in times, public keys and some non-secret account data, plus tokens from some invitation and recovery emails.

Do I need to change my password?

1Password says no action is needed. The main thing is to watch for phishing that mentions 1Password.

How many people were affected?

1Password has not said.

Is this like the LastPass breach?

It began in a similar way, with stolen source code. In LastPass's case a second attack later took encrypted vault backups. 1Password says its vaults were not reached.
