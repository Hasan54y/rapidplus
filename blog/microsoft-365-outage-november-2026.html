Microsoft 365 Outage: Why Exchange Online Went Down for 24 Hours in November 2026

A hidden bug, a traffic surge and failed safeguards. The hour-by-hour story of the Microsoft email outage, who was hit, and how to cope next time.

Rapid+ Team · 23 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

On this page

Written on 23 November 2026. The timeline comes from Microsoft's preliminary post-incident report as described by The Register, and from IBTimes Australia, which summarises Microsoft's statements. We did not read Microsoft's own report, and a final version was due on 20 November. Details may change.

For about a day, from 12 to 13 November 2026, millions of people could not reliably use Outlook or the email behind it. Microsoft's Exchange Online went down for users around the world, and many Microsoft 365 services that depend on it were hit with it. Microsoft says it was not an attack. It was a defect in a July software change that sat unnoticed until a surge of traffic set it off.

This article sets out what happened hour by hour, what Microsoft says went wrong, who was affected, and what a normal business or person can do so that the next outage costs less.

The short version

  • The outage ran from roughly 16:00 UTC on 12 November to 16:10 UTC on 13 November, a little under 24 hours, with Microsoft's final customer update on 14 November.
  • Exchange Online was the main casualty. Many users saw it as Outlook not working, and email delivery was delayed for many.
  • Microsoft's first statement called it "a technical issue not related to cyber or AI". Its report says there was no attack.
  • The cause was a defect in a July 2026 change to the Microsoft 365 network gateway servers, triggered when Teams traffic moved to the same gateways.
  • Rolling back was not possible because the defect was months old. Engineers built an update that reverted the faulty change and applied it to servers in groups.

What happened, step by step

The Register notes that engineers spent about 15 hours working on the problem before the cause was pinned down. IBTimes gives the outage as 23 hours and 50 minutes, though it also describes it as lasting "more than a day", so treat the exact length as approximate.

What Microsoft says went wrong

Microsoft's report, as relayed by The Register and IBTimes, describes three problems that stacked on each other.

  1. A hidden defect. A change made in July 2026 to the network gateway servers contained a bug that caused lock contention inside an internal Windows process, which used up the machine's processor and memory. It stayed dormant. Microsoft said its load testing focused on weaker hardware with fewer cores, and the problem is worse on newer machines with more cores.
  2. A traffic surge. When Teams failed over to the primary gateway, the load landed on servers already running with about 14 percent of capacity out of service for an unrelated reason.
  3. A cascade. The barriers meant to stop one group of servers failing from overloading the rest did not hold. At the worst point, more than 80 percent of the servers were affected.

Charles Lamanna, a Microsoft executive, summed it up on X in the same three points, according to IBTimes.

How it was fixed

A normal rollback would not work, because the defect had been present since July and the code from before it was long gone. Microsoft instead built an update that returned the faulty code path to a known good state. Engineers disconnected servers in batches, applied the update, restarted them and brought them back. This is why recovery took hours even after the cause was known.

Who was affected

IBTimes reports that the outage touched more than 70 countries, and that Microsoft 365 has about 430 million users. It lists downstream effects including hospitals and clinics in Britain's NHS, a hospital network in Toronto, courts in the Philippines and Singapore, a Japanese government service and staff in the US House of Representatives. Anderson Economic Group estimated losses of about $7 billion. That is an outside estimate, not a figure from Microsoft.

The Register and IBTimes do not mention service credits or compensation. Microsoft apologised to users as the outage ended.

Why "not an attack" matters

When a big service goes down, rumours about hackers start within minutes. This time they were wrong, and Microsoft said so early. Still, an outage and an attack feel the same to the person who cannot read their email. The practical difference is data: nothing in the reports suggests that anyone's messages were stolen. Be careful with emails claiming to be from Microsoft about "restoring your account" after an outage, because scammers use these moments. Our guide to spotting fake pages applies here too.

How to cope with the next outage

  1. Know the status page. Microsoft publishes service health on its admin centre and on social channels. Bookmark them before you need them.
  2. Keep a second way to reach people. A chat group, a phone tree or a different email address for critical contacts costs nothing.
  3. Save what you need offline. Recent calendar entries, key contacts and important documents should not live in one place only.
  4. Do not retry in a loop. Hammering a failing service adds load. Wait for the status update.
  5. Review dependencies. A business that runs email, files and meetings from one provider has one point of failure.

Frequently asked questions

Was the Microsoft outage a cyberattack?

No. Microsoft said it was not related to cyber, and its report describes a software defect from a July change. It says it ruled out an attack quickly.

How long did the outage last?

About 24 hours of disruption, from around 16:00 UTC on 12 November to 16:10 UTC on 13 November. Microsoft issued a final update on 14 November.

Which services were hit?

Exchange Online, which many people experience as Outlook, was the main service. Other Microsoft 365 services that depend on it were affected. Teams, OneDrive and SharePoint could fail away from the faulty gateway and were less affected.

Did I lose any email?

The reports describe delayed delivery and access problems, not lost mail. IBTimes said Microsoft reported the email backlog processed after recovery. If you think a message is missing, ask the sender to resend it.

Will I get compensation?

The reports we read do not mention credits. Business customers should check their service agreement and ask their account contact.
