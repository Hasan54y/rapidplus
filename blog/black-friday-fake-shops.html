Black Friday 2026: How to Spot a Fake Online Shop Before You Pay

The padlock proves little and typos no longer give scams away. A five-step check you can do in two minutes, using advice from the FTC.

Rapid+ Team · 18 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 18 November 2026. The core advice comes from the US Federal Trade Commission's guidance on shopping online. Black Friday falls on 27 November 2026. Security vendors publish many scam lists at this time of year and some of their statistics are marketing, so we use only practical advice that does not depend on their numbers.

The week around Black Friday is when fake online shops do their best business. The sites look professional, the prices look like bargains and the ads appear in your social feed. Spelling mistakes used to give them away. They are no longer a reliable sign, and a padlock in the address bar proves even less. This article gives you a short routine that works even when the page looks perfect.

The short version

  • Pay with a credit card where you can. The FTC says it best protects you if something goes wrong, and gives you the right to dispute charges.
  • Never buy from a seller that only accepts gift cards, wire transfers, payment apps or cryptocurrency.
  • The padlock and "https" mean the connection is encrypted. They do not mean the shop is honest.
  • Search the shop's name with the words "complaint" or "scam" before you order.
  • Type the address of a shop yourself or use a bookmark, and do not rely on links in texts, emails or ads.

How fake shops work

A fake shop is a website built to take your money or card details and deliver nothing, or something worthless. It usually appears through an ad on social media or a search result, offers a famous product at a price far below normal and may copy a real brand's logo and photos. Some also use a spoofed copy of a real shop's login page to collect passwords.

Tools that generate whole websites in minutes have made the pages look better. An Aston University fraud researcher, quoted in a news report, says that signs such as spelling mistakes and poor design are no longer reliable. We therefore focus on what the site does, not how it looks.

What the padlock means

The FTC states it directly: "The 's' after http means the site is encrypted, but it doesn't mean it's a legitimate site." Encryption stops others from reading what passes between you and the site. It does not tell you who owns the site. Scam sites use HTTPS too, because certificates are cheap and automatic. Our article on Chrome's HTTPS warning explains what the padlock does and does not promise.

A five-step check before you buy

  1. Look at the address. Read the whole domain, not only the start. A name like "brand-sale-outlet.shop" is not the brand's site. Open the real brand's own website yourself and see if the deal is there.
  2. Search for the seller. The FTC suggests searching the seller or product name along with "complaint" or "scam". Do not rely on star ratings alone, because the FTC warns that some reviews and ratings are fake or misleading.
  3. Check how you are asked to pay. A shop that insists on gift cards, wire transfers, payment apps or cryptocurrency is a red flag. The FTC says: "Never buy anything from online sellers that insist you can only pay with gift cards."
  4. Read the delivery and return terms. The FTC says sellers must ship as promised in their ads, and if no time is promised, within 30 days of receiving your order and payment. Check the return window, who pays for return shipping and any restocking fee.
  5. Keep records. Save receipts, order confirmations, return policies and your messages with the seller. If something goes wrong, they are your evidence.

Why the card matters

The FTC says paying by credit card "best protects you and your money in case of a scam, or if something else goes wrong". Card holders can dispute charges for double billing, goods not received or wrong or defective items. The FTC sets a 60-day window from the first bill showing the error, so act quickly. A debit card, bank transfer or payment app may not give the same chances of getting money back.

To limit your exposure, some people use a card with a low limit for new shops or a virtual card number from their bank. These are personal choices and not part of the FTC advice we quote.

Using the browser to your advantage

  • Use bookmarks for shops you trust. It avoids look-alike addresses.
  • Do not enter a password by following a message. Delivery and refund texts are a common lure. Open the shop's app or type the address yourself.
  • Keep shopping in its own profile. A separate browser profile keeps your shopping cookies and logins away from your email and banking.
  • Use a password manager and passkeys where offered. A password manager offers a saved password only on the real domain, which exposes a spoofed login page. Our article on passkeys explains why they are harder to phish.
  • Update your browser. Security fixes matter more when you are visiting many unfamiliar sites. See our article on the latest Chrome zero-day.

Frequently asked questions

Is a site with https and a padlock safe?

Not necessarily. The padlock means the connection is encrypted, not that the seller is honest. The FTC says the same thing in plain words.

What is the safest way to pay?

The FTC says a credit card best protects you if there is a scam or another problem. Avoid sellers that only accept gift cards, wire transfers, payment apps or cryptocurrency.

How can I check if a shop is real?

Search its name with "complaint" or "scam", check the full domain and look for contact details, delivery terms and a return policy. Do not trust star ratings alone, because some are fake.

I paid a fake shop. What now?

Contact your card issuer or bank at once and ask to dispute the charge. The FTC gives a 60-day window for credit card disputes. Keep your records, change any password you entered and report the site to your national consumer authority.

Are deals on social media ads trustworthy?

Treat them with extra care. Fake shops are often promoted through ads. Go to the brand's official website directly and look for the offer there.
