CrowdStrike buys Zscaler in large 3D letters beside a phone showing Value, all stock $43.9B

The device security giant is buying a leader in cloud network security. The numbers, the AI pitch and the race with Palo Alto Networks.

Rapid+ Team · 28 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 28 November 2026. The deal terms come from the companies' joint announcement filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission on 23 November, with market reaction from CNBC and context from TechCrunch. The deal has not closed and still needs shareholder and regulatory approval.

CrowdStrike, the company best known for protecting laptops and servers, has agreed to buy Zscaler, a leader in cloud network security, in an all-stock deal worth about $43.9 billion. It is CrowdStrike's biggest acquisition and one of the largest in cybersecurity history. Here is what the deal is, why the companies say they are doing it, and what it could mean for the businesses and people who rely on their products.

The short version

  • CrowdStrike will pay 1.135 of its shares for each Zscaler share, about $260.41 a share, a premium of roughly 34% to Zscaler's 20 November close.
  • The deal values Zscaler at about $43.9 billion. CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84.1% of the combined company.
  • Zscaler founder Jay Chaudhry will keep running the Zscaler business and join CrowdStrike's board.
  • It is expected to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal year 2028, subject to Zscaler shareholder and regulatory approval.
  • On the day of the announcement, CNBC reported CrowdStrike shares fell 9% while Zscaler's jumped nearly 18%.

Who the two companies are

CrowdStrike secures computers, laptops, phones and other devices with its Falcon platform. Many people know the name from the July 2024 global IT outage caused by a faulty CrowdStrike update.

Zscaler sits between users and the internet. Its Zero Trust Exchange checks every connection rather than trusting anything inside a company network. The announcement says Zscaler protects half of the Fortune 500, runs more than 200 service locations and processes more than 750 billion transactions a day.

In short, CrowdStrike protects the device and Zscaler protects the connection. The two have been partners, with more than 2,000 shared customers and 16 integrations today.

Jay Chaudhry and his affiliates, who hold about 34% of Zscaler's shares, have agreed to vote in favour.

Why they say they are doing it

The pitch is one platform: "one platform, one data layer, one contract". After closing, CrowdStrike plans to offer Zscaler's network security, data protection and AI security as part of Falcon, sold under a single Falcon Flex contract. It also says about 8,000 Zscaler customers do not use CrowdStrike yet, an obvious sales opportunity.

AI is the headline theme. "We see a path to become the largest AI safety and security company the world has ever seen," CrowdStrike chief executive George Kurtz said. Chaudhry called the two companies "complementary" and the deal "the natural evolution of our longstanding partnership." A JPMorgan note quoted by CNBC said Zscaler could bring "more breadth in an era of vendor consolidation."

The race with Palo Alto Networks

CNBC frames the deal as CrowdStrike closing the gap with Palo Alto Networks, the market leader. It reports the two companies were worth more than $265 billion combined at Friday's close, against Palo Alto's $282 billion. It follows other big consolidation deals CNBC lists: Google and Wiz, Cisco and Splunk, and Palo Alto and CyberArk.

Earnings on the same day

Both companies reported quarterly results alongside the announcement. CNBC says CrowdStrike earned 34 cents a share on $1.58 billion of revenue in its fiscal third quarter, ahead of the 31 cents and $1.53 billion analysts expected according to LSEG, and that Zscaler's results also beat estimates. The companies held a joint call on the morning of the announcement in place of separate calls. Investors still marked CrowdStrike down on the day, a common reaction for the buyer in a large all-stock deal, because existing shareholders end up owning a smaller slice of a bigger company.

What we do not know yet

  • Which regulators will review it. The announcement only says "required regulatory approvals" and names none.
  • The exact closing date. The companies say the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028. TechCrunch puts it more loosely at around this time next year.
  • What happens to overlapping products. The companies describe a single platform but have not said which products, if any, will be retired.
  • George Kurtz's role after closing is not spelled out in the announcement, although he remains chief executive today.

What changes for customers and users

  • Nothing yet. Until the deal closes, the companies say they will "continue to operate independently."
  • Fewer vendors, more eggs in one basket. Buying device and network security from one company can be simpler and cheaper, but it also concentrates risk, a point the 2024 outage made painfully clear.
  • Brand unclear. The announcement does not say whether the Zscaler name will remain.
  • Approval risk. Regulators still have to approve a deal this large, and the companies list approvals among the risks.

For ordinary internet users, the effect is indirect but real: these two companies protect much of the corporate world, including many of the banks, shops and services you log into every day.

Frequently asked questions

How much is CrowdStrike paying for Zscaler?

About $43.9 billion in stock, or about $260.41 per Zscaler share based on CrowdStrike's 20 November closing price.

When will the deal close?

The companies expect it in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal year 2028, after Zscaler shareholder and regulatory approval.

Will Zscaler's CEO stay?

Yes. Jay Chaudhry will continue to lead the Zscaler business and join CrowdStrike's board.

How did the stock market react?

CNBC reported CrowdStrike shares fell 9% on the day, while Zscaler's rose nearly 18%.

Does this affect my devices?

Not directly. It mainly affects businesses that use either company's products. Nothing changes until the deal closes.
