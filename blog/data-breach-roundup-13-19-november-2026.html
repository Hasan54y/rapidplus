Data Breach Roundup: 9 Incidents From 13 to 19 November 2026 and What to Do

All nine breaches in one table, with what was exposed and what is only a claim, plus a short plan for when a breach letter arrives.

Rapid+ Team · 23 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 23 November 2026. This is built on Privacy Guides' weekly roundup of 20 November, which summarises reporting by BleepingComputer, TechCrunch and WIRED. We did not read the original company filings. Many numbers below are estimates or claims, and we mark them. Where a number appears only in a headline, we say that too.

Nine data breaches were reported between 13 and 19 November 2026. Together they touch drivers, cinema-goers, patients, bank customers and commuters. Some figures are confirmed in official filings, and some are only claims. This roundup lists all nine in one place, shows what was exposed in each, and ends with a short plan for what to do when a breach letter lands in your inbox.

We have separate reports on Uber and Cursor, Transport for London and the supplier breaches at Infoblox, TSMC and Wellington IT. The three below are the ones we have not covered in depth.

Fandango: card details taken at checkout

Fandango Media, the US ticketing platform that sells for about 31,000 cinema screens, including AMC, Regal and Cinemark, says payment card details typed into its official website's checkout pages were exposed between 8 and 11 October. The exposed data includes the card number, security code, expiry date, billing ZIP code and email. Fandango did not say how many people were affected and is notifying customers who bought through its own site. Nothing in the roundup names who did it.

This kind of attack, in which code on a checkout page copies what you type, is often called card skimming on the web. The same method appears in our report on the Optimizely attack. If you bought tickets there in that window, ask your bank to watch your card or replace it.

SBC Medical Group: 1.6 million people

SBC Medical Group manages clinics, mainly in Japan, that receive about 7 million patient visits a year. In an SEC filing, the company said two cyber incidents exposed customer and employee information, with the breach on 16 November. It put the number at 1.6 million people. The data includes full names, contact details, dates of birth, patient card numbers, member IDs, passwords and survey responses. The roundup adds that hundreds of thousands of further records were taken, covering reservation requests, treatment information, customer reviews, employee records and a booking platform. "Hundreds of thousands" is approximate. Patients should change passwords for any related account and watch for messages that mention clinic visits.

CreditNirvana: a debt-collection platform

CreditNirvana, owned by the Indian fintech Perfios, runs an AI platform for debt collection. According to WIRED, two serious vulnerabilities could have exposed sensitive data, including credit reports, ID cards, case details, legal documents, emails, texts and call transcripts. The flaw is fixed, but the exposure potentially lasted years, and the roundup says three corporate customers were affected. The headline of the WIRED article puts the scale at 6.6 million accounts and 315,000 call recordings. That figure appears only in the headline in the summary we read, and the roundup describes exposure, not confirmed mass theft.

What to do when a breach letter arrives

  1. Do not click links in it. Open the company's website yourself or use a phone number you looked up. Scammers send fake breach notices.
  2. Find out what was taken. A letter should say. Passwords and card numbers need fast action. Names and addresses need caution against phishing.
  3. Change the password for that service, and for any other account that shared it. Use a password manager so each one is different.
  4. Replace the card if card details were taken. Your bank can do it quickly.
  5. Look into a credit freeze if ID numbers were exposed, where your country offers one.
  6. Watch your accounts for a few months. Breach data is often sold and used later.
  7. Turn on a passkey or two-step verification for email first, because it resets everything else. See our passkey guide.

Frequently asked questions

Which breach this week is the biggest?

SBC Medical Group reports 1.6 million people in an SEC filing. TfL's estimate is about 265,000, and Uber's is 149,346 drivers. CreditNirvana's headline figure is 6.6 million accounts but is not confirmed in the text we read.

Are these numbers confirmed?

Some are, like Uber's count from a state filing and SBC's from an SEC filing. Others are attacker claims, such as TSMC's 46,000 records, or estimates, such as Cursor's 5,000 developers.

I bought Fandango tickets in October. Am I affected?

Only if you bought through Fandango's official website between 8 and 11 October. Fandango says it is notifying those customers. Ask your bank to watch the card.

How do I know whether my data is in a breach?

Companies must usually tell you. You can also check a breach-notification service with your email address, and treat messages about it with care.

What is the first thing to do after a notice?

Change the password on that service, and on any other account that used the same one. Then watch for phishing.
