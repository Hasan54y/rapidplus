TfL Oyster Data Breach: 265,000 Customers May Be Affected, What to Do Now

Names, addresses, dates of birth and security answers may be exposed. What TfL says, and the steps to take if you hold an Oyster photocard.

Rapid+ Team · 23 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

Written on 23 November 2026. This is based on BleepingComputer's report of 19 November, which draws on customer breach notices and TfL's public page. We did not find a direct TfL statement we could quote, so most of this is paraphrased. TfL's investigation is not finished and its numbers may change.

Transport for London says details of around 265,000 Oyster photocard customers may have been exposed after attackers got into its email systems. TfL found out on 16 November 2026, when customers reported getting emails that asked them to create an account, sent from TfL's own mail servers. If you hold an Oyster photocard, here is what was exposed, what was not, and what to do now.

The short version

  • TfL discovered the incident on 16 November after customers received emails asking them to create an account. The emails came from TfL's own systems because of a compromise.
  • About 265,000 Oyster photocard customers may have had account details exposed, up from an early estimate of about 250,000. The number could still rise.
  • Possibly exposed: full names, phone numbers, email addresses, home addresses, dates of birth, and security questions and answers.
  • TfL says passwords, bank and payment card details and journey information were not compromised, and it has found no evidence of misuse so far.
  • Oyster photocards still work for travel. The photocard website is offline for maintenance.

What happened

The first sign was not a TfL announcement but customers' inboxes. People who hold Oyster photocards, the discounted cards for students, older people and some other groups, received emails telling them to create an account. They looked genuine because they were sent through TfL's real mail systems, which attackers had compromised.

TfL says it has contained the incident, contacted affected customers, begun sending breach notifications and informed the UK Information Commissioner's Office. The Oyster photocard website has been taken offline.

Security questions and answers

The security questions are the part people overlook. If you use the same answers on other sites, such as your first school or your mother's maiden name, anyone holding them could try them there.

The phishing risk is the real danger

A leaked list of names, addresses, birthdays and phone numbers is exactly what scammers need to write convincing messages. Expect emails and texts that claim to be from TfL, your bank or "the police". A caller who knows your address and date of birth sounds trustworthy, and that is the trick. The emails that started this incident are a reminder that even a real sender address does not prove a message is safe.

What to do if you hold an Oyster photocard

  1. Ignore the account-creation email that TfL says to disregard. Do not click links in it.
  2. Do not reply to calls or texts that ask for personal details, even if they quote your address. Hang up and use a number you looked up yourself.
  3. Change your security answers on every other site where you used the same ones. A password manager can store made-up answers.
  4. Change passwords on other services that used the same information as your TfL account.
  5. Watch for the prompt. When the photocard portal comes back, TfL says all customers will be asked to change their password and security questions.
  6. Report anything suspicious to TfL and to your bank.

Your card remains valid, and if you need to renew it or check journey history, TfL Customer Services can help in the meantime.

What follow-up scams usually look like

After a breach, criminals do not always attack the stolen account. They use the details to build messages that feel personal. For an Oyster breach, common patterns would be:

  • A "refund" or "fare adjustment" message that says you were overcharged and asks you to enter card details to receive money.
  • A "your photocard has been suspended" text with a link to renew it. Real renewals go through the official TfL site that you type in yourself.
  • A call from "your bank" or "the police" that quotes your address and date of birth to prove it is real, then pushes you to move money.
  • A "verify your account" email that asks for your security answers. A company that really needs them would not ask by email.

These are general patterns that follow breaches of this type. They are not reports of specific messages sent after this incident. The safe habit is the same each time: stop, do not use the link or number in the message, and contact the organisation through an address you found yourself.

Why security questions are a weak lock

Security questions ask for things that are not secret at all: a school, a first pet, a birth town. Friends, social media and now data leaks can supply the answers. If your answers were in the TfL data, treat them as public. The best fix is to stop answering truthfully. Invent a long random answer for each site and save it in a password manager, so that a leaked answer from one place is useless anywhere else. Where a site offers a passkey or an authenticator app instead, use that. Our passkey guide explains why they resist this kind of leak.

Background: TfL was hit before

BleepingComputer notes that in 2024 members of the Scattered Spider group breached TfL and stole customer data. It says that attack cost TfL £29 million, and that two hackers pleaded guilty in June 2026. The current incident is separate and no group has been named for it.

Frequently asked questions

Was my Oyster card balance or payment information stolen?

TfL says bank and payment card details were not compromised. The data that may have been exposed is personal details such as name, address, date of birth and security questions.

How do I know if I am one of the 265,000?

TfL says it is contacting affected customers and sending breach notifications. If you hold an Oyster photocard, watch for that message, and treat any other email about it with care.

Can I still use my Oyster photocard?

Yes. TfL says photocards remain valid for travel. The photocard website is offline while the problem is fixed.

Should I change my password?

TfL says passwords were not compromised, but it will ask all photocard customers to change their password and security questions when the portal returns. Changing passwords on other sites that share your answers is sensible.

Has the data been misused?

TfL says it has found no evidence of misuse so far. The investigation is not complete.
