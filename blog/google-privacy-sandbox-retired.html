Google Retires the Privacy Sandbox: What It Means for Chrome, Cookies and Your Privacy

Years of work on cookie replacements ended for lack of adoption. What is gone, what stays in Chrome and how to block trackers yourself.

Rapid+ Team · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 24 November 2026. This is based on Google's own Privacy Sandbox announcement of 17 October 2025 and its status page, last updated on 20 November 2026. A vendor blog also claims Chrome version numbers and dates for the removal. Google's pages give none, so we leave those out. Google has not said third-party cookies will be removed.

Google has abandoned most of the Privacy Sandbox, the set of tools it spent years building to replace tracking cookies in Chrome. Topics, Protected Audience and Attribution Reporting are retired, and Google's status page now lists most of them as "Deprecated and removed". The reason Google gives is simple: not enough people used them. For people who browse, the real question is what is left, and what still tracks you.

The short version

  • On 17 October 2025 Google announced that it would retire most Privacy Sandbox technologies, after evaluating feedback about their expected value and their low levels of adoption.
  • Its status page, updated on 20 November 2026, lists Topics, Protected Audience, Attribution Reporting, Shared Storage and Related Website Sets as deprecated and removed in Chrome.
  • Some privacy features stay, including CHIPS, FedCM, Private State Tokens and bounce tracking mitigations.
  • Google has said it will keep its current approach of giving users a choice about third-party cookies in Chrome. It has not announced removing them.
  • The status page gives no removal dates or Chrome version numbers.

What the Privacy Sandbox was

For years, advertisers followed people across sites with third-party cookies. Google planned to remove them from Chrome and offer replacements that kept advertising working without tracking individuals. Topics would label your browser with a few broad interests such as "cooking". Protected Audience would let ads be chosen inside your browser. Attribution Reporting would measure whether an ad led to a purchase, without naming you.

In the end few companies adopted them, which is the reason Google gives for ending the project.

What is retired

Google's October 2025 post lists these as retired: Attribution Reporting API for Chrome and Android, IP Protection, On-Device Personalization, Private Aggregation (including Shared Storage), Protected Audience for Chrome and Android, Protected App Signals, Related Website Sets, SelectURL, SDK Runtime, and Topics for Chrome and Android.

On Android, the page lists Attribution Reporting, On-Device Personalization, Protected App Signals, Protected Audience, SDK Runtime and Topics as scheduled for phase-out. Google says documentation will stay on its site for reference.

Why Google says it did this

The only reason in Google's post is that after evaluating ecosystem feedback about their expected value and in light of their low levels of adoption, it decided to retire them. The same post says CHIPS and FedCM have seen broad adoption. Google also says it wants to support an attribution standard being developed at the W3C. Third parties have offered other explanations, such as regulatory pressure and technical complexity, but those are analysts' opinions, not statements from Google.

What about third-party cookies?

This is the point most readers want answered. Google's October 2025 post does not say third-party cookies will be removed. It refers back to an earlier statement that Chrome will maintain its current approach to offering users third-party cookie choice. Neither the post nor the status page announces a removal. A vendor blog says cookies stay and that Google will not show a standalone prompt, but that claim comes from that blog and not from Google's pages.

In practice, that means a site can still try to set third-party cookies in Chrome unless you block them. Other browsers have blocked them for years.

What still tracks you

  • Cookies. Third-party cookies still work in Chrome unless you turn them off.
  • Logins. If you sign in to a service, it knows who you are across its own sites.
  • Fingerprinting. Sites can combine details about your device into a recognisable profile without any cookie. See our guide to browser fingerprinting.
  • Data brokers and apps. Tracking does not stop at the browser.

What you can do now

  1. Block third-party cookies. In Chrome, open Settings, Privacy and security, then Third-party cookies, and choose to block them.
  2. Use tracking protection. A content blocker stops many trackers before they load.
  3. Keep sessions separate. A separate browser profile for shopping and banking limits what one site can link.
  4. Clear cookies on a schedule, or use private windows for one-off visits.
  5. Review what you share when a site asks for consent. Reject optional tracking.

What it means for advertisers

With the replacement tools gone, advertisers lose one route to measuring ads without cookies. Analysts expect more use of first-party data, meaning information that customers give a company directly, and of measurement done on the company's own servers. That is opinion, not a Google statement. For readers, the practical effect is that more of the tracking industry depends on logins and fingerprints, which are harder to block than cookies.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Privacy Sandbox over?

Most of it is. Google's status page lists Topics, Protected Audience, Attribution Reporting and others as deprecated and removed. Some privacy features, such as CHIPS and FedCM, continue.

Is Chrome removing third-party cookies?

Google has not announced that. Its October 2025 post says it will maintain its current approach of offering users a choice about third-party cookies.

Why did Google retire the tools?

Google says it evaluated feedback about their expected value and their low levels of adoption.

Does this make me less private?

Not directly, because most people never used the replacements. But third-party cookies remain possible in Chrome unless you block them, so check your settings.

Which browsers already block third-party cookies?

Several other browsers block them by default. Check your browser's privacy settings to see what it does.
