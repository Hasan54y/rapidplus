UK Forced to Reveal Secret Apple Encryption Order: What the Ruling Means

The order is still secret, but its existence is now acknowledged. What the tribunal decided, what Apple says, and what end-to-end encryption means for you.

Rapid+ Team · 23 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

On 19 November 2026 a UK tribunal ruled that the British government can no longer hide the fact that it ordered Apple to weaken the encryption protecting iCloud. The order itself is still secret, and Apple has refused to comply. But for the first time the government has been forced to admit a secret order of this kind exists, and the case that forced it will go on into 2027.

This article explains what was ordered, what the ruling changes, what is still unknown, and what encryption on your own phone and cloud backups means in practice.

The short version

  • The order is called a Technical Capability Notice under the UK's Investigatory Powers Act. Liberty says such notices can require companies to change products to help surveillance, including by weakening encryption.
  • It targets Apple's Advanced Data Protection (ADP), the setting that puts iCloud backups under end-to-end encryption, so that even Apple cannot read them.
  • The Investigatory Powers Tribunal rejected the Home Office's argument that confirming the notice would harm national security, according to TechCrunch.
  • Liberty and Privacy International brought the case, and Liberty puts the number of UK iCloud users at an estimated 39 to 40 million, based on a Which? estimate.
  • Apple welcomed the decision, and has said it has never built a backdoor or master key and never will.
  • A further hearing is expected in 2027.

What was ordered

The exact contents of the notice have not been made public. Liberty says plainly that the details have not been revealed. What is known comes from leaks and reporting. TechCrunch says the notice was reportedly a demand that Apple build a way for UK authorities to reach customer data anywhere in the world, as leaked a year earlier. It says the order was later narrowed to cover the data of British nationals stored in Apple's encrypted cloud, and that Apple challenged the second order too.

Until this ruling, Apple was legally barred from even saying that a notice existed. That is why the earlier coverage relied on leaks.

What Advanced Data Protection is

Most cloud backups are encrypted, but the company holds the key, so it can read the data if the law requires it. With end-to-end encryption, only your own devices hold the key. The company stores scrambled data it cannot open, and cannot hand it over in readable form even when ordered to. That is the whole point, and also the reason governments push back: a company that cannot read your data cannot help investigators read it.

TechCrunch reports that Apple blocked UK users from switching on ADP rather than comply with the order, and links to its February 2025 coverage of that step. Neither page we read says whether the block is still in place, so we cannot tell you whether ADP is currently available in the UK.

What the tribunal decided

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal handles complaints about state surveillance. Liberty and Privacy International, acting with two individuals, asked it to reject the government's "neither confirm nor deny" position on the order. According to Liberty, a September 2026 hearing took place and the tribunal has now agreed. Liberty's director Akiko Hart called the judgment "a victory for transparency".

The ruling does not decide whether the order is lawful, and it does not release the order's text. A final hearing on the wider power to issue such notices is expected in 2027, and TechCrunch says a hearing on what details can be released is anticipated next year as well.

What is still unknown

  • The actual terms and scope of the notice, including the narrowed second order.
  • Whether ADP is blocked for UK users today.
  • The status of Apple's challenge to the second order.
  • The Home Office's position. TechCrunch says it has yet to confirm the notice exists and did not comment.
  • Whether any more detail will ever be published.

The exact date of the tribunal's judgment is not given on either page, beyond TechCrunch's reference to a Thursday ruling, which fits 19 November.

Why it matters beyond the UK

TechCrunch says US officials and lawmakers criticised the order, arguing it puts Americans' privacy at risk. The reasoning is that an encryption weakness built for one country's investigators is a weakness for everybody, because a system cannot tell good access from bad. Security experts have made that argument for years, and it is the main point of the case.

What you can do

  1. Check your own settings. If you use an iPhone, look for the Advanced Data Protection option in your Apple account settings. Whether you can turn it on may depend on where you live.
  2. Use end-to-end encrypted messaging for private conversations, and know which apps offer it by default.
  3. Keep a local backup of what matters to you on a drive you control, encrypted with a password you hold.
  4. Protect the account itself. Strong encryption does not help if someone can sign in as you. Use a passkey or a long unique password and two-step verification.
  5. Know what a VPN does not do. It does not change what a provider can read in your cloud backup. Our proxy and VPN explainer covers the limits.

Frequently asked questions

Did Apple give the UK a backdoor?

Apple says it has never built a backdoor or master key and never will, and TechCrunch says it refused the order. Reports say it stopped offering ADP to UK users instead, but we could not confirm the current position.

What is a Technical Capability Notice?

It is an order under the UK Investigatory Powers Act that can require a company to change its products or services to help the state gain access to data. Liberty says it can include removing or weakening encryption.

Is my iCloud data readable by Apple?

It depends on your settings. With Advanced Data Protection on, most iCloud data is end-to-end encrypted and Apple cannot read it. With it off, Apple holds keys for much of the data.

Does the ruling make the order illegal?

No. It only stops the government from refusing to confirm that the order exists. A later hearing will consider the wider powers.

How many people are affected?

Liberty says an estimated 39 to 40 million people in the UK use iCloud, citing a Which? estimate. How many of them used ADP is not stated.
