Ransomware Up 38%: What Beazley's Q3 2026 Report Says and How to Protect Yourself

Exploited software flaws doubled and attackers now steal logins that are already open. The report in plain words, with steps for people and small firms.

Written on 23 November 2026. The figures come from Security Today's report of 19 November on Beazley Security's quarterly threat report for the third quarter of 2026. We did not read Beazley's report itself, and the article does not give the baseline for the 38 percent rise or an average ransom, so we do not state either.

Ransomware is growing again, and the way in has changed. Beazley Security says reported ransomware incidents rose 38 percent in the third quarter of 2026. The share of cases that started with an exploited software flaw doubled to 28 percent. Attackers also increasingly steal a login that is already open instead of guessing a password. Here is what the report says in plain words, and what an ordinary person or small business can do about it.

The short version

  • Reported ransomware incidents rose 38 percent in the third quarter, according to Beazley Security's report as covered by Security Today.
  • Cases beginning with an exploited vulnerability doubled to 28 percent, up from 14 percent in the second quarter.
  • Vendors shipped almost 80 percent more software updates, and published CVEs reached nearly 37,000.
  • Healthcare remained the most targeted industry, with 21 percent of investigated cases.
  • Malware accounted for 19 percent of initial breaches, up from 14 percent, driven mainly by a trick called ClickFix.
  • Attackers are stealing active login sessions and even registering their own devices.

Flaws are the front door

A year ago, stolen passwords were the usual way in. Now more attacks begin with a known software flaw that was not patched in time. Beazley says security teams faced a nearly 80 percent jump in vendor updates, with CVEs reaching close to 37,000. More flaws means more chances to fall behind, and attackers move fast on the ones that matter.

The targets are often self-hosted, internet-facing apps such as build servers, analytics tools and collaboration software. Attackers who get in often find cloud access keys on the compromised server and use them to deploy ransomware through the victim's own cloud tools.

Beazley's advice, quoted by Security Today, is to prioritise known exploited vulnerabilities and internet-facing applications first. That is the same advice behind our reports on the Chrome zero-day and on the Outlook flaw under attack.

ClickFix: you run the attack yourself

ClickFix is a trick in which a web page shows a fake error or CAPTCHA and tells you to fix it by pressing a key combination, then pasting something into the Windows Run box or a terminal. What you paste is a command that installs malware. Because you did it yourself, some security checks do not see it as an attack.

Beazley says malware was behind 19 percent of initial breaches, up from 14 percent, mostly because of ClickFix. Extortion or ransomware typically followed an infection by two to three weeks, so the damage is not immediate and people forget the earlier odd page.

The rule: no real website needs you to paste a command to prove you are human. If a page tells you to, close it.

Stealing the login that is already open

Beazley says attackers increasingly steal active login sessions instead of passwords. They use tools that sit between you and a real site, device-code phishing, and malware that copies saved sessions from your browser. Josh Carolan of Beazley says attackers are "waiting until users authenticate to hijack active sessions". Some also register their own device, so they keep access after you reset your password.

This matters for a browser user. A session is the small piece of data that keeps you signed in. If a script or malware copies it, the attacker is signed in as you, and a strong password does not help. Our report on the Optimizely attack shows how a script on a page can reach cookies.

AI agents on the attacking side

The report describes an attack run almost entirely by coordinated AI agents. It got past patching and two-step verification, exploited a flaw in a custom application and used a service account credential to move across the network and steal data. Francisco Donoso of Beazley says the agents work "tirelessly to locate unpatched servers and flat networks faster than human attackers", which makes basic network hygiene and segmentation more important.

What an ordinary person can do

  1. Update everything quickly. Browser, operating system, Office, router and phone. Turn automatic updates on.
  2. Never paste commands from a web page. Treat any fake CAPTCHA that asks for it as an attack.
  3. Use passkeys where available. Beazley recommends phishing-resistant sign-in such as passkeys. See our passkey guide.
  4. Review where you are signed in. On key accounts, look at the list of devices and sessions, remove ones you do not know and sign out of the rest.
  5. Keep an offline backup. A copy that is not connected to your computer cannot be encrypted by ransomware.
  6. Use a separate browser profile for banking and work, so one infected session cannot reach everything. Our profiles article explains how.

What a small business can do

  • List what is reachable from the internet and patch those systems first.
  • Turn off device-code sign-in where you do not need it, and block new sign-in registrations from unmanaged devices, as Beazley recommends.
  • Keep cloud access keys out of servers and rotate them.
  • Split the network, so a break-in on one machine does not give access to everything.

Frequently asked questions

How much did ransomware grow?

Beazley Security says reported incidents rose 38 percent in the third quarter of 2026. The article we read does not say what that is compared with.

What is ClickFix?

It is a fake error or CAPTCHA page that tells you to paste a command into your computer to fix a problem. The command installs malware.

Which industry is hit most?

Healthcare, with 21 percent of the cases Beazley investigated.

Can two-step verification be bypassed?

Some methods can, for example when attackers steal an already authenticated session or trick you into approving a device code. Beazley recommends phishing-resistant sign-in such as passkeys.

What is the single best thing to do?

Install updates quickly, especially for anything reachable from the internet, and never run commands that a web page tells you to paste.
