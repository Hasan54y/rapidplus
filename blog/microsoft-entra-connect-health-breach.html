Microsoft Entra Connect Health Breach: Admin Emails Exposed, What It Means and What to Do

A July bug, a November fix and a list of admin addresses in the wrong hands. What was exposed and how administrators should respond.

Rapid+ Team · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 24 November 2026. This is based on BleepingComputer's report of 21 November, which cites a Microsoft admin notification sent on 20 November. We did not read the notification itself. Microsoft declined to give further details, and it has not named the attackers or said how many organisations were affected.

Microsoft has told administrators that attackers exploited a flaw in Entra Connect Health, a monitoring service for company identity systems, and read information about their organisations. The data includes the email addresses of administrators and technical contacts. Microsoft says it found no changes to configurations and that passwords were not exposed. The flaw is fixed on Microsoft's side. The risk that remains is targeted phishing aimed at the people whose addresses were taken.

The short version

  • The flaw was in the Entra Connect Health API. It was introduced on 23 July 2026, found by Microsoft on 9 November and fixed on 19 November. Microsoft finished its investigation on 20 November.
  • Attackers had unauthorised read-only access to tenant-level metadata. Microsoft says it found no unauthorised configuration changes.
  • Exposed: administrator and technical contact email addresses, tenant IDs, display names, domain names, country codes, licence tiers and sync status.
  • Not exposed, per Microsoft: passwords, password hashes, authentication tokens, valid cryptographic keys, on-premises server and Active Directory layouts, and Azure subscription information.
  • Microsoft says no software updates or customer action are required to close the hole, and recommends watching for phishing.

What Entra Connect Health is

BleepingComputer describes Entra Connect Health as a cloud-based service that lets administrators monitor the health, performance and alerts of their on-premises identity systems from the Microsoft Entra admin center. Many companies use Microsoft's cloud for sign-in and also keep their own servers. This service helps them watch the link between the two.

The reporting does not say when attackers first used the flaw, so the exposure window could be anywhere from July to November.

What the attackers could read

According to Microsoft's list, as reported:

  • Email addresses of tenant technical contacts and administrator accounts.
  • Tenant IDs, display names, initial domain names, country codes and regional information.
  • Entra licence tiers and directory sync status, including whether sync was on and the time of the last sync.
  • Connect Health onboarding status.
  • An expired certificate that had been used to register Connect Health agents with a Microsoft internal service decommissioned in late 2024. It was no longer in use but was still returned by the API.

The article says the flaw could have allowed changes to Connect Health monitoring settings, but Microsoft found none.

Why metadata matters

Names and domains do not sound dangerous, but they are what a convincing scam is made from. An attacker who knows who your administrators are, which licence tier you use and when your directory last synced can write an email that sounds like it comes from Microsoft support. Administrators have powerful accounts, so a single successful phish can lead to a full takeover. That is why Microsoft's main advice is about phishing, not about fixing a hole.

Who was affected

Microsoft has not said how many organisations were affected or who exploited the flaw. BleepingComputer says more than a dozen administrators posted on Reddit that they had received the notice, and some managed service providers reported notices for several customer tenants. If you administer a Microsoft 365 tenant that uses Connect Health, look in the Azure Service Health notifications for your tenant.

What administrators should do

  1. Read the notice in Azure Service Health, and tell your security team.
  2. Use phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication for privileged accounts, such as passkeys or hardware security keys. See our passkey guide.
  3. Review your Connect Health configuration and check for anything you did not set.
  4. Check Entra audit and sign-in logs for suspicious activity.
  5. Warn the people whose addresses were exposed. Tell technical contacts to expect fake support messages.
  6. Use Microsoft's script at aka.ms/ConnectHealth/scripts to see which types of tenant metadata could have been exposed. It shows current values, not what was actually read.

What staff and customers should know

  • Do not trust a message just because it knows your company name or domain.
  • Microsoft will not ask for your password or a code by email or phone. Treat any such request as a scam.
  • If an "administrator" asks you to approve a sign-in or install software, check through another channel first.

A familiar pattern

This follows the same story as the Microsoft 365 outage earlier in the month: a change made in July sat unnoticed until November. It also shows why a flaw in a vendor's service can reach many customers at once, as in the supplier breaches we covered. Microsoft's own November updates also fixed an Outlook flaw under attack.

Frequently asked questions

What was exposed in the Entra Connect Health breach?

Administrator and technical contact email addresses, tenant IDs, display names, domain names, country codes, licence tiers and directory sync status. Microsoft says passwords, hashes, tokens and keys were not exposed.

Has it been fixed?

Yes. Microsoft says it deployed a server-side fix on 19 November and that no software updates or customer remediation are needed.

Who was behind it?

Microsoft has not said. It also has not said how many tenants were affected.

Were configurations changed?

Microsoft says it did not identify any unauthorised configuration changes. The attackers had read-only access.

What is the real risk now?

Targeted phishing and social engineering aimed at the exposed administrators and contacts. Microsoft recommends alerting security teams and using phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication.
