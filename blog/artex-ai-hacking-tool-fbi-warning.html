Artex: The AI Hacking Tool the FBI Says Breaches Networks in Under Three Hours

ConnectWise, Barracuda and South Korean banks were hit. The FBI warning on an AI tool that finds simple weaknesses faster than people can respond.

Rapid+ Team · 24 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 24 November 2026. This is based on TechCrunch's report of 16 November on an FBI advisory about an AI hacking tool named Artex. We did not read the FBI advisory itself. TechCrunch does not list the FBI's recommendations, so the advice section is our own reading of the weaknesses described, and we say so.

The FBI has warned that criminals and spies are using Artex, an open-source tool that runs penetration tests with AI agents, to break into real networks. In some cases it took under three hours from picking a target to being inside. Victims include the IT software firm ConnectWise, the security company Barracuda and several South Korean financial institutions. The common thread is not clever tricks. It is simple weaknesses that were left open.

The short version

  • Artex is one of several open-source platforms that use AI models to probe networks. It can be connected to hosted models, open-source models and non-US models.
  • The first public release was in July. The developer later made it closed-source, but the FBI says the earlier open version still circulates and is widely available.
  • The FBI says attackers have targeted financial services, government, technology, defence, phone and internet companies, and law firms.
  • Artex was used against several South Korean financial institutions, stealing personal data of at least 68,000 people, according to the FBI as reported by TechCrunch.
  • In some cases, Artex-linked attacks went from targeting to active compromise in under three hours.

What Artex is

A penetration test is a controlled attack that a company pays for, to find its weak spots before a real attacker does. Artex does this with AI agents. It scans a network quickly, finds simple bugs, exposed passwords or stolen passwords, uses them to get a foothold and then spreads to databases and mailboxes. The same thing that makes it useful to defenders makes it useful to criminals. TechCrunch notes that after the attacks the developer made the software closed-source, but the earlier open version is still easy to get.

The FBI says Artex intrusions can be noisy. They create a lot of traffic and data that can trigger alerts. But the tool moves so fast that human defenders can find it hard to keep up.

The victims named

ConnectWise

TechCrunch says Microsoft previously reported that hackers, whom it believes are likely Chinese, used Artex to break into ConnectWise and reach the Microsoft 365 cloud environments of at least 2,250 of its customers. The FBI confirmed this. A ConnectWise spokesperson told TechCrunch the company cannot confirm the number of affected environments.

Barracuda

Barracuda disclosed that financially motivated criminals breached it in an Artex-linked attack. The tool found an exposed credential belonging to a third-party service that Barracuda had not revoked after it stopped working with that vendor. Attackers used it to reach credential stores that Barracuda uses to collect data from customers' systems. Barracuda said its logs were not detailed enough to rule out access, so it changed all customer credentials. Its security chief, Arve Kjoelen, wrote that defenders who rely on spotting and responding to malicious activity "will often arrive after the attacker has finished".

South Korean financial institutions

The FBI says Artex was used against several South Korean financial institutions, with the attacks blamed largely on weak defences and missing protections such as multi-factor authentication. At least 68,000 people had personal data stolen. TechCrunch also links an earlier article of 6 November, which says researchers found hundreds of Artex systems exposed on the internet without a password, including the one used against South Korea.

What "under three hours" means

TechCrunch says that in some cases Artex-linked attacks went from targeting a system to active compromise in under three hours. It is a speed claim about some cases, not an average, and the article gives no method or sample size. It also does not say whether the clock starts at the first scan or the first contact. The point still stands: an attack can move faster than many companies' response plans, which often measure time in days.

What these attacks have in common

  • Old, forgotten access. Barracuda's entry point was a credential for a vendor it no longer used. Nobody had switched it off.
  • Missing second factor. The South Korean cases lacked multi-factor authentication.
  • Thin logs. Barracuda could not tell what had been touched, so it had to assume the worst.
  • Exposed tools. Some of the attackers' own Artex servers were open to anyone on the internet.

None of these needs a new technology to fix. They need housekeeping. That echoes our report on supplier breaches, where leftover access and thin logging also played a part.

What defenders can do (our reading)

TechCrunch does not list the FBI's recommendations. These steps follow from the weaknesses it describes and should be read as our inference.

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere, and prefer phishing-resistant methods such as passkeys. Our passkey guide explains how they work.
  2. Revoke access when a vendor leaves. Keep a list of every outside service that holds a key to your systems.
  3. Rotate credentials that may have been exposed, and remove passwords from code and storage.
  4. Keep detailed logs so that you can say what was and was not accessed.
  5. Fix the simple things first. AI tools find the easy bugs fastest.
  6. Plan for speed. Decide in advance who can cut off access in the first hour.

What it means for everyone else

If you are not running a company network, the practical message is that automated attacks lower the cost of going after small targets too. Use a unique password for each service, a password manager and two-step verification on email. Keep software updated, as in our guide to the latest Chrome update.

Frequently asked questions

What is Artex?

It is an open-source platform that uses AI agents to test the security of networks. The FBI says criminals and spies are using it to attack real organisations.

Who has been attacked with it?

TechCrunch names ConnectWise, Barracuda and several South Korean financial institutions. The FBI says targets also include government, defence, technology and law firms.

Is the tool still available?

The developer made the software closed-source after the attacks, but the FBI says the earlier open-source version is still widely accessible.

Does AI make hacking much faster?

In some cases, yes. TechCrunch reports compromises in under three hours. It is not an average, and the article does not explain how it was measured.

How can I reduce my risk?

Use multi-factor authentication, unique passwords and prompt updates. Companies should also remove old vendor access and keep detailed logs.
