New York City Votes to Ban Surveillance Pricing: What the Bill Says

The same product, a different price for you. What the New York City bill actually bans, what stays allowed and which browser habits feed the data behind it.

On this page

Written on 18 November 2026. This is a news explainer and not legal advice. Outlets differ on some details, such as the start date and how many places have similar laws, and we mark those points below. Check the city's own record for the final text.

On 12 November 2026 the New York City Council voted 44 to 5 to ban "surveillance pricing", the practice of using a person's data to set a personal price for the same product. The bill still has to become law, and a similar state bill is waiting for the governor. Whatever happens next, the vote puts a name on something many shoppers have suspected: that the price you see online can depend on who the seller thinks you are.

This article explains what the bill says, what it does not cover, and which of your browser's habits feed the data that such pricing depends on.

The short version

  • The City Council passed Introduction 891-A by 44 to 5. It was sponsored by Speaker Julie Menin, and Mayor Zohran Mamdani supports it.
  • It bars businesses from using algorithms that draw on personal data to set individualised prices for the same goods or services.
  • Bona fide, transparent discounts and rewards are allowed. The Record, which quoted the bill, lists loyalty programs and veteran and student discounts.
  • The city's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection could fine violators up to $1,000 per violation, according to The Record.
  • Reports differ on timing. Route Fifty says the ban takes effect one year after the bill becomes law, while another report gives 120 days.
  • New York State's legislature passed a similar bill, the One Fair Price Act, in June. It awaits Governor Kathy Hochul's signature.

What surveillance pricing means

Dynamic pricing is old and mostly accepted: airlines and hotels raise prices when demand is high. Surveillance pricing is different. The price depends on you, not on the market. A seller may use what it knows about your location, your device, your browsing history, your past purchases or a profile bought from a data broker to estimate how much you will pay, and then show you a number to match.

The bill, as described by Route Fifty, bars businesses from using algorithms that draw on New Yorkers' personal data to set individualised prices. Menin said: "They can't mine people's personal data to charge them a different price for the exact same good." The Record gives examples of the data at issue: search history, what users hover over online and data broker profiles about spending habits and demographics.

What stays allowed

The sponsors say the bill does not stop discounts. Menin said that any suggestion companies cannot offer discounts is "flatly incorrect". The Record reports that the bill carves out "bona fide discounts and rewards that are legitimate and transparent", including loyalty programs and veteran and student discounts. Ordinary price changes that apply to everyone, such as a weekend sale or a rise during a busy season, are a different thing from a price built for one person.

Business groups did raise concerns. Route Fifty reports that Uber, whose services fall under the bill, objected, and a Times Union article it links says business groups have lobbied the governor to change the state version. Whether the city bill will cause problems for loyalty programs in practice is something regulators and courts will decide.

Who else has acted

The Record reports that Seattle passed a similar ban that was signed last month, and that New Jersey, Connecticut and Maryland already have bans. Route Fifty counts New York City as the fifth jurisdiction to act. Other outlets give different numbers, including calling it the second city after Seattle, so treat any count as approximate. The pattern is clear even if the number is not: local and state governments are moving faster than national lawmakers on this subject.

The state bill is the one to watch. It passed in June and has been on the governor's desk for months. The Record says advocates fear the governor could use a power to amend passed bills to weaken it. Another outlet noted that the governor has until the end of the year to sign or veto it.

What we do not know

The Record notes that the companies allegedly using surveillance pricing have not been confirmed, and gives the example of Kroger, which denies using it. The bill is a rule against a practice, not proof that the practice is widespread. If you have noticed a different price on another device or account, that is an anecdote, and prices can differ for ordinary reasons such as stock levels, delivery location or a coupon that applied to one account.

What your browser contributes

Pricing built on personal data needs inputs. Some come from the shop itself, such as your account and purchase history. Others travel with your browser.

  • Cookies and identifiers. Third-party cookies and ad IDs link your visits across sites.
  • Your device and location. The user agent string, screen size and IP address reveal what you use and roughly where you are. Our article on user agent strings shows how much a single header says.
  • Fingerprinting. Sites can combine many small traits into a recognisable profile without cookies, as we describe in the article on browser fingerprinting.

Reducing these inputs is reasonable privacy hygiene. We are not claiming that it changes the price you are shown, because that has not been demonstrated in the reporting we read. What you can do is limit the data trail:

  1. Use a separate browser profile for shopping so that your other browsing does not mix in.
  2. Block third-party cookies and turn on tracking protection.
  3. Compare an important price in a private window or on another device, and screenshot any large difference.
  4. Be careful with loyalty apps, which tie purchases to your identity.

Frequently asked questions

Is the New York City ban in force now?

No. The council has voted, but reports say it takes effect after the bill becomes law, and they differ on whether the delay is 120 days or one year. Check the city's legislative records for the current status.

Does it ban all personalised pricing?

No. It targets algorithmic prices set from personal data. Legitimate and transparent discounts and rewards, such as loyalty programs and student or veteran discounts, are allowed according to The Record.

What is the penalty?

The Record reports fines of up to $1,000 per violation, enforced by the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection.

Does it apply outside New York City?

No. Other places have their own rules. Seattle, New Jersey, Connecticut and Maryland are reported to have bans, and a New York State bill awaits the governor's signature.

Can I tell whether a shop is personalising my price?

Not reliably. Prices change for ordinary reasons. You can compare the same item across devices, a private window and a logged-out session, and keep screenshots, but a difference alone does not prove it.
