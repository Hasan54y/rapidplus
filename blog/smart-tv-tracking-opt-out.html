Do Smart TV Tracking Opt-Outs Work? What a New Study Found

A university team measured what TVs send after you opt out of viewing tracking. Which brands stayed quiet, which did not, and what limits the findings have.

Rapid+ Team · 18 Nov 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

Written on 18 November 2026. The findings come from a Northeastern University study called "Watching the Watchers", as summarised by Malwarebytes on 5 November 2026. We did not read the full paper. The researchers could not see inside encrypted traffic, so the study shows that data kept flowing, not what it contained.

Smart TVs can identify what is on their screen with a feature called automatic content recognition, or ACR, and sell what they learn to advertisers. Every major brand has a setting to turn it off. A new study tested whether those settings work, and for two big brands the answer was only partly.

This is not a browser story, but it is the same story that runs through this blog: tracking you did not notice, a setting that looks like control, and a way to check whether the control is real.

The short version

  • Researchers at Northeastern University tested twelve smart TVs from ten manufacturers, covering eight operating systems that power about 95% of US smart TVs.
  • They ran more than 5,000 automated sessions across ten viewing scenarios, each with ACR on and with the opt-out selected.
  • Four TVs went completely silent after the opt-out: LG (webOS), Sony (Google TV), Amazon (Fire TV) and Supersonic (VIDAA).
  • Vizio kept sending what looked like ACR traffic after the opt-out for first-party streaming and antenna TV. Samsung (Tizen) did so for some apps, HDMI input and first-party streaming.
  • Because most ACR traffic is encrypted, the researchers inferred it from where it went, when and how much, not from its contents.

What ACR is

ACR captures audio, video or both from what your TV is showing and uploads it to a third party, usually the manufacturer. The recipient turns it into a digital fingerprint, matches it against a catalogue and learns what you are watching. This works for any source: a streaming app, an antenna, a cable box or a game console on an HDMI port. The result can support targeted advertising and audience measurement.

The setting is often found in the privacy or terms menus under names such as "viewing information", and the wording differs from brand to brand.

What the researchers found

Brand (system)After opting out
LG (webOS)No ACR traffic seen
Sony (Google TV)No ACR traffic seen
Amazon (Fire TV)No ACR traffic seen
Supersonic (VIDAA)No ACR traffic seen
VizioACR-like traffic continued for first-party streaming and antenna TV. None seen for third-party streaming.
Samsung (Tizen)ACR-like traffic continued in some runs: about 7% for Netflix, 10% for YouTube, 20% for Tubi, 60% of HDMI sessions and all first-party streaming sessions.

Malwarebytes also reports two details that deserve care. The researchers could not inspect Vizio's traffic to an Amazon endpoint, so they saw no change after the opt-out but could not confirm whether it breaks Vizio's 2017 settlement with the FTC. And Vizio's WatchFree+ service sent unencrypted titles of the shows or channels being watched, which users cannot turn off, according to the researchers.

The VIDAA system was an exception to the encrypted pattern: it sent data unencrypted, and its servers, which needed no login at the time, exposed more than 57 million ACR detection results from other people's TVs, according to the Malwarebytes summary.

What the study does not prove

  • It shows traffic, not contents. Encryption hid the payload. The researchers inferred ACR traffic from the destination, timing, volume and protocol.
  • It tested particular TVs. Twelve sets cannot represent every model and software version, and software updates can change behaviour in either direction.
  • It does not prove a legal violation. That is for regulators and courts to decide.

The honest reading is that an opt-out is not guaranteed to stop everything, and that this can be checked by measuring network traffic.

What you can do

  1. Turn the setting off anyway. The researchers say the opt-out appears to be effective on the LG, Sony, Amazon and Supersonic sets in the study. On other brands it may reduce tracking even when it does not remove it.
  2. Use an external streaming device. One option the researchers suggest is to disconnect the TV from the internet and use a separate streaming stick. The TV then has no network path to send anything.
  3. Block known ACR servers at your router. The researchers published a blocklist on GitHub for this purpose. It takes some technical comfort to use.
  4. Treat app sign-ins carefully. Streaming apps on the TV tie your viewing to an account regardless of ACR.

The link to your browser

The TV problem and the browser problem look alike. In both, a company collects behavioural data, offers a setting and asks you to trust that it works. In the browser, you can see more: the developer tools show requests, and a content blocker shows what it stops. Frequently asked questions

What is ACR on a smart TV?

Automatic content recognition is a feature that captures audio or video from the screen and uploads it, usually to the manufacturer, which identifies what is playing. It can support targeted advertising.

Which TVs honoured the opt-out in the study?

LG (webOS), Sony (Google TV), Amazon (Fire TV) and Supersonic (VIDAA) showed no ACR-like traffic after the opt-out. Vizio and Samsung did not fully honour it.

Does this prove Vizio and Samsung are tracking me?

It shows that traffic consistent with ACR continued after the opt-out. Because the traffic is encrypted, the study cannot say what data it carried.

Is it safe to disconnect my TV from the internet?

Yes, but you lose its built-in apps and updates. An external streaming device can fill that gap.

Where is the study?

It is called "Watching the Watchers", from Northeastern University researchers. Malwarebytes links to the project page, which is in the sources below.
