Online Age Verification: How It Works and What It Costs Your Privacy

Age checks are meant to protect children but need data from everyone. A plain look at the methods, the arguments on both sides and your options.

Rapid+ Team · 18 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

On this page

Written on 18 November 2026. This article draws on the Electronic Frontier Foundation's page on age verification, which argues against such laws. We state its arguments as the EFF's and mark where laws and court decisions are reported by it. Supporters of these laws make a different case, which centres on protecting children, and we describe it where we can. Legal details change quickly, so check current law for your place.

More websites now ask you to prove your age before they show you anything. The laws behind it are meant to protect children, and many people agree with that goal. Critics, including the Electronic Frontier Foundation, say the methods turn every visit into an identity check. This article explains how age checks work, what each method collects, and what you can do to limit the cost to your own privacy.

The short version

  • Age-verification laws require some websites and apps to confirm that a user is old enough. They started with adult content and have spread to social media and other services.
  • The EFF says that every method needs "sensitive and immutable personal information" that connects a person's offline identity to their online activity.
  • The EFF's list of methods: government ID checks, biometric face scans, algorithmic or behavioural monitoring and digital ID. It says that age estimation tools are often inaccurate and invasive.
  • Everyone is checked, not only children, because a site cannot know who is a child without testing all users.
  • Courts are in the middle of the argument. The EFF reports that in October 2026 two federal appeals courts allowed Florida and Georgia to enforce laws restricting young people's access to social media.

What the laws do

The details differ by place, but the pattern is the same: a service in scope must take steps to find out whether a user is an adult, or in some laws whether a user is over a particular age such as 16, and block or limit those who are not. The EFF lists several recent developments:

  • It says that in October 2026 two federal appeals courts allowed Florida and Georgia to enforce laws that restrict young people's access to social media.
  • It says California's AB 1709, signed by the governor on 10 September 2026, works as a ban on social media use for people under 16.
  • It mentions the US Supreme Court decision in FSC v. Paxton, which concerns age verification, without giving details on the page we read.

Other countries are moving too, and some are building government-backed apps or digital wallets for age checks. We did not verify the details in primary sources, so we do not describe them.

How age checks work and what they collect

MethodWhat it needsThe privacy question
Government ID checkA photo of an ID, sometimes with a selfieName, birth date and ID number go to a service. Who stores them and for how long?
Face scan (age estimation)A camera imageBiometric data. The EFF says estimation is often inaccurate.
Behavioural or algorithmic checksBrowsing, posting and device signalsMonitoring everyone to guess ages.
Digital IDA credential in a wallet or appCan reveal only "over 18", if designed that way, but depends on the system.

The table is a summary of the EFF's list and of how these methods generally work. Designs differ: a check can be done on your device, by the website or by a third-party vendor. The more it is done by a third party that retains data, the greater the risk of leaks. The EFF points to several breaches of age-verification providers and gives no counts for how many.

The two sides

Why supporters want it

Supporters argue that children can easily reach content and social platforms that harm them, and that sites have too little incentive to keep them out. Age checks put a barrier at the door.

Why critics object

The EFF argues that the laws need sensitive data from everyone, that the data can be leaked, hacked or misused, and that the systems act as censorship tools: they can block people from seeing or sharing information that the government treats as harmful. It adds that age gates burden the expressive rights of adults and young people, and that they benefit large platforms while harming smaller ones. The page does not detail these last claims.

Both sides describe the same facts. They disagree about whether the benefit is worth the cost, and about whether a design can protect children without building a database of who looks at what.

What you can do

  1. Ask what is collected and kept. Before you upload an ID or a selfie, read what the service says about storage. Prefer methods that confirm your age and discard the image.
  2. Use the least revealing option. If a site offers several methods, an on-device check or a credential that says only "over 18" reveals less than a full ID upload.
  3. Do not reuse the same identity everywhere. Keep accounts that need verification in a separate browser profile, as described in our article on browser profiles.
  4. Be careful with look-alike pages. Scammers imitate age-check screens to collect ID photos. Check the address and only verify on a site you meant to visit. Our article on fake shops lists address checks that apply here too.
  5. Watch for tracking you did not expect. Verification can add new trackers and third-party calls to a page. A content blocker will show some of them.
  6. Take extra care with children's accounts. If you manage a child's device, built-in family settings can be a more private way to restrict access than uploading documents to many sites.

What about VPNs?

Some people respond to a check by using a VPN to appear in another region. That can change which version of a site you reach, but it is not a privacy solution, and some laws and services try to detect it. A VPN also moves trust to the provider, as we explain in the article on proxies and VPNs. Do not use a VPN to break the law where you live.

Frequently asked questions

Why do adults have to prove their age?

A site cannot tell who is a child without checking all users, so the checks apply to everyone who reaches the gated content.

What does the EFF object to?

It says each method requires sensitive personal information that links offline identity to online activity, that the data can be leaked or misused, and that the laws can act as censorship and burden the speech of adults and young people.

Are these laws in force?

Some are. The EFF reports that two federal appeals courts allowed Florida and Georgia to enforce laws restricting young people's social media access in October 2026, and that California's AB 1709 was signed on 10 September 2026. Others face court challenges, so check the status where you live.

Is face-based age estimation accurate?

The EFF says age estimation tools are often inaccurate and invasive. Accuracy depends on the system and on the person.

Can a check be private?

It can be less invasive. A credential that proves only that you are over a certain age, checked on your device, reveals less than uploading an ID to a third party. How private it is depends on the design and on what is stored.
